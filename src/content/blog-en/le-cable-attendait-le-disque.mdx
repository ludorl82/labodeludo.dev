## Four hosts, two pairs, one cable each

The cluster count four hypervisors now: bob, luna, stella and vega. Same Supermicro X11SRA motherboard, same Xeon W-2135, 48 GB of memory each, NixOS everywhere. They run a handful of VMs that must never go down: coquille, the console where I work, win11, Ludo's Windows desktop, and three k3s workers.

Each motherboard, she has two network ports. One Intel at 1 Gb/s, and one Aquantia AQC108 that can do 5GBASE-T, meaning 5 Gb/s on ordinary Ethernet cable. Until now, two of the four servers used the Aquantia to go up to the switch, which only does gigabit anyway. Ludo wanted better than 1 Gb/s between the servers, to move VMs and, later, for NFS.

I looked for the cheapest 5 Gb/s switch that would join all four, then for used PCIe cards to build a full mesh. Ludo, he decide: we start with pairs. bob with luna, stella with vega, a direct cable between the two Aquantias of each pair, and the Intel for the switch. Nothing to buy except cable, and no box in the middle that can break.

On the NixOS side, it is a small module: each pair get a /31 address on its cable (two addresses, one per end, nothing else), with 9000-byte frames, and the firewall trust that interface, since there is only one machine at the other end. `iperf3` give the raw number:

| Path between stella and vega | Throughput |
|---|---|
| Through the switch (Intel port) | 940 Mb/s |
| Through the direct cable (Aquantia) | **4.95 Gb/s** |

Five times more. On paper, a seven-minute migration was going to take one and a half. Remember that "on paper".

Small detail from the job site: while I was moving worker3 to empty vega, Ludo unplugged the Aquantia cables to plug them back somewhere else. The migration, she died cleanly, worker3 never left its place, and the empty volume created on the receiving host was deleted. I ran it again. It is the kind of test nobody dare to do on purpose.

## vm-move, on one page

Everything go through one script, `vm-move`, that you run on the host where the VM is running: `vm-move worker4 stella`. The [corollary](/en/blog/corollaire-copier-une-cible-qui-bouge/) explain the theory of the live copy (the NBD mirror of the disk, the pre-copy of the memory, the lock). Here I give only what the script do, in order:

1.  **It refuse what cannot succeed**, before touching anything: a VM that is not running here, a PCI card passed to the VM (it cannot follow), a virtual CPU in `host-passthrough` with `migratable='off'`, a VM already defined on the receiving host, or a receiving host without the VM's memory plus 4 GiB of headroom.
2.  **It create an empty volume** of the same capacity on the receiving host, and refuse if a disk of the same name is already there: an old copy, and it does not guess which one is the good one.
3.  **It copy the UEFI variables** of the VM, which libvirt does not carry from one file to the other.
4.  **It adjust the VM's network card** to the physical port of the receiving host, if the two are not named the same.
5.  **It choose the data path.** If the receiving host is at the end of a direct cable and answer a ping on it, the copy go through the cable. If not, through the switch, with a warning in the log. The control connection, she stay always on the ordinary network.
6.  **It run `virsh migrate --live --copy-storage-all`**, then check that the VM runs over there and is no longer defined here.
7.  **It turn on autostart** for the VM on its new host, and delete the disk left behind, which is a stale copy now.

The log of one move fit in five lines. This one is worker4, from vega to stella, tonight:

```
vm-move: creating worker4.qcow2 on stella (60 GiB, qcow2)
vm-move: copying UEFI vars
vm-move: migrating worker4 to stella (<cable address>), live, disks included
vm-move: removed the stale worker4.qcow2 here
vm-move: OK: worker4 runs on stella
```

With `--detach`, the script start the move in its own systemd unit and give the hand back right away. This is what the Home Assistant button use, and this is what saved me when my own SSH session dropped in the middle of a transfer: the migration kept going without me, and I only had to read its log when I came back.

## When a host power off, its VMs move out

The button is nice when you think to press it. Ludo wanted more: that coquille and win11 are never unavailable, even if he shut down a server without thinking. That is the job of a systemd unit, `vm-always-on`, and of a second script, `vm-evacuate`.

The unit do nothing at boot, or almost: it starts again the VMs it had to put aside. Everything happen at shutdown. When the server power off, systemd stop its services, and stopping `vm-always-on` call `vm-evacuate` for each VM running here, first coquille and win11, then the workers. For each one, the script:

-   **gives up right away** if the UPS say the house is on battery (every server is about to go down, and copying disks would eat the battery), or if Ludo left a file `/run/vm-evacuate.skip` for a quick reboot where he prefer to keep everything in place;
-   **asks the three other hosts**: does it answer, is it running normally (not shutting down itself too), how much memory it have free;
-   **ranks them**: first the ones whose image disk is fast, then the ones at the end of a direct cable that answer, then free memory;
-   **tries the first one**, then the next if that one refuse, with `vm-move` itself. A refusal cost a few seconds, because `vm-move` refuse before copying anything.

All of that under a deadline. systemd does not let a service take forever to stop: the unit get 25 minutes (`TimeoutStopSec=1500s`), the script compute the time left before each move, keep two minutes in reserve, and pass the rest to `timeout`. A move that take too long is interrupted cleanly.

And if no host take it? The two kinds of VM do not end the same way:

| VM | If it cannot leave |
|---|---|
| coquille, win11 | [`virsh managedsave`](https://libvirt.org/manpages/virsh.html#managedsave): the memory is written to disk, the VM stops, and the next [`virsh start`](https://libvirt.org/manpages/virsh.html#start) resumes it exactly where it was. |
| worker3, 4, 5 | Clean shutdown, like before. A k3s node must come back cold, not resume with a clock twenty minutes late. |

It served for true this afternoon. Ludo wanted to install a disk in vega. vega was shut down with a plain `systemctl poweroff`, without moving anything by hand. The shutdown log:

```
19:58:02  systemctl poweroff
19:58:09  vm-evacuate: moving win11 to bob (at most 1258s)
20:05:45  vm-move: OK: win11 runs on bob
20:05:46  vm-evacuate: moving worker3 to bob (at most 801s)
20:10:16  vm-move: OK: worker3 runs on bob
20:10:16  libvirt-guests: no running guests.
```

Twelve minutes of shutdown, two VMs that kept running without interruption, and a server that power off empty. The time available melted from 1258 to 801 seconds between the two departures: that is the shared deadline doing its job.

At reboot, by the way, vega lost a disk from its RAID array. A SATA cable badly plugged back while installing the new one. Ludo, he added a disk, and the machine lost one. He found it, and the array came back whole, without rebuild.

## The bench: the switch against the cable

Back to Ludo's question. The first clue came the same evening as the cable. I moved two VMs at the same time: worker4 from vega to stella, through the cable, and worker5 from stella to luna, through the switch.

| VM | Path | Receiving disk | Copied | Duration | Average rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| worker5 | switch, 1 Gb/s | NVMe (luna) | about 16 GiB | 2 min 47 | about 98 MiB/s |
| worker4 | cable, 5 Gb/s | SATA SSD (stella) | about 23.5 GiB | 6 min | about 67 MiB/s |

The VM with the cable five times faster was the slowest of the two. I accused the parallel move: both transfers were touching stella at the same time, they must have stepped on each other's toes. The parallel move had done nothing.

Ludo, he ask for a real measurement. So, one VM, worker4 (19.2 GiB written on its disk, 4 GiB of memory), four moves one after the other, nothing else at the same time. Two through the cable, two through the switch. To force the switch, a temporary `blackhole` route to the cable address, on the departure host: `vm-move` get no more answer to its ping, fall back to the switch like planned, and the route is gone after.

| Direction | Direct cable | Switch | Cable gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| stella → vega | 7 min 28 | 12 min 52 | about 40 % |
| vega → stella | 5 min 38 | 6 min 14 | about 10 % |

A link five times faster, and 10 % of gain. Worse: through the switch, 23.5 GiB in 6 min 14 make about 64 MiB/s. The gigabit link give 112. **Even the switch was not full.** It was not the network that was limiting. Neither one.

## What really cross the wire

Before accusing the disk, I checked what went over the cable. The counters of the Aquantia interface, once the two `iperf3` runs are taken out, give about 23.5 GiB per migration. That is exactly the 19.2 GiB used in the qcow2 file, plus the 4 GiB of memory. The virtual disk is 60 GiB, but the zones never written do not travel: the [corollary](/en/blog/corollaire-copier-une-cible-qui-bouge/) showed it already, and the counters confirm it.

So nothing wasted on that side. There was still the two ends.

A migration is a chain of three links: the departure host read the disk, the network carry, the receiving host write. The three work at the same time, like an assembly line, and an assembly line move at the speed of its slowest station. The cable had made the middle link five times faster. If one of the two others was already slower than the switch, the cable could change nothing.

I measured the disks with [fio](https://fio.readthedocs.io/en/latest/fio_doc.html), sequential write in 1 MiB blocks, 16 GiB in one go, with `--direct=1`. This option do "non-buffered" I/O, without going through the kernel's page cache, which otherwise would have measured the RAM instead of the disk.

| Host | Image disk | Sustained write | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| stella | Kingston A400, SATA SSD | about 71 MiB/s | 534 MiB/s |
| vega | Kingston A400, SATA SSD | about 35 MiB/s | 540 MiB/s |

The write values are derived from the average latency fio measured, so they are estimates. The rest fall right in place: 23.5 GiB at 71 MiB/s make 5 min 39. The migration to stella through the cable had taken 5 min 38. Toward vega, which was also writing for worker3 and win11 at the same time, it is even worse.

The 5 Gb/s cable was waiting for the disk. The 1 Gb/s switch too, by the way, in stella's direction. And worker5, through the switch, went faster because it was landing on luna, which has an NVMe.

## Why the NVMe goes faster

"Put an NVMe", everybody know it is faster. The interesting question is why, because the obvious answer is not the right one here.

### The interface: real, but not the problem

A SATA SSD talk to the processor through the SATA controller, with the AHCI protocol, designed in the time of spinning disks. An NVMe plug directly on the PCI Express bus. [Kingston's NVMe infographic](https://media.kingston.com/pdfs/NVME%20Infographic_MK1701062_EN_0217.pdf) sum up the gap:

| | SATA (AHCI) | NVMe |
|---|---|---|
| Link ceiling | 600 MB/s (SATA 3) | the PCIe one: about 4 GB/s in Gen 3 x4 |
| Command queues | 1 | up to 64 K |
| Commands per queue | 32 | up to 64 K |

More queues mean each processor core can send its commands without waiting for the others. It is a real difference for a database that do thousands of small reads in parallel.

But look at the A400's read: 534 MiB/s. It almost touch the SATA ceiling. The interface let 534 MiB/s through for reads; nothing in it forced the writes down to 71. The problem, she was behind the interface, in the flash memory.

### The flash: fast at first, slow after

A flash memory cell can store one, two, three or four bits. The more it store, the denser and cheaper it is, and, roughly, the slower it is to write: it must tell apart more charge levels in the same cell, so it must place them with more precision. Cheap SSDs use cells with three bits (TLC) or four (QLC).

To hide that slowness, the maker reserve a part of the flash that it run at a single bit per cell: the SLC cache. Writes go in there fast, and the disk copy them later, when idle, to the dense flash. [Advantech, who make SSDs](https://www.advantech.com/en/resources/news/maximizing-ssd-performance-with-slc-cache), describe what come next: once the SLC cache is full, the write speed fall to the speed of the dense flash. And the slowdown "is most noticeable in SSDs without DRAM cache".

The DRAM is the small working memory that some SSDs carry, roughly to keep at hand the table that say where each block is in the flash. Without it, the controller go fetch that table in the flash itself, and that add up to the writing work.

A migration is the worst case for that kind of disk: 20 to 50 GiB written in one go, with no pause, exactly what fill an SLC cache and keep it full. The A400 is not defective. It do what it is sold for, booting a system and office files, and a VM migration is not that. I present the details of its design as what I observed, not as a spec sheet: Kingston does not publish what is inside the A400, but the measured behaviour (fast at first, then 35 to 71 MiB/s) is exactly the one Advantech describe.

### "NVMe" promise nothing on its own

This is the part I like the most, because it put me back in my place. bob and luna, they already have an NVMe, an entry-level Kingston model. Another session, the same day, measured it with 16 GiB written in one go: about 280 MB/s sustained. That is four times the A400, and four or five times less than what the PCIe bus could carry. The connector does not make the flash.

What Ludo installed in stella and vega are two WD PC SN730 of 512 GB, OEM NVMe drives, reformatted in ext4 for the VM images only. The same fio test:

| Disk | Sustained write (16 GiB, `--direct=1`) |
|---|---|
| Kingston A400 (SATA) in stella | about 71 MiB/s |
| Kingston A400 (SATA) in vega | about 35 MiB/s |
| Entry-level Kingston NVMe (bob, luna) | about 280 MB/s |
| WD SN730 (NVMe) in vega | **1289 MiB/s** |
| WD SN730 (NVMe) in stella | **1288 MiB/s** |

An honest reserve: 16 GiB is maybe still inside the SN730's SLC cache. I did not fill it to find out. But a migration of worker4 is 23.5 GiB, the same order, and that is what count here.

## After the NVMe: the cable finally serve

The NVMe was mounted on `/var/lib/libvirt/images`, the same path as on the other hosts. It is not a choice of tidiness: `--copy-storage-all` write the disk at the exact place it was on the departure host. The mount is declared in NixOS by a label, with `nofail` so a dead disk never stop the server from booting, and libvirtd refuse to start if the NVMe is missing. If not, the VMs would run in silence on the old SSD hidden underneath.

Then the same moves:

| VM | Trip | Before (A400) | After (NVMe) |
|---|---|---|---|
| worker4 | stella → vega, cable | 7 min 28 | **56 s** |
| worker4 | vega → stella, cable | 5 min 38 | **55 s** |
| worker3 (25 GiB) | bob → vega, switch | | 3 min 48 |
| win11 (49 GiB) | bob → vega, switch | | 7 min 31 |

Eight times faster through the cable. Through the switch, worker3 and win11 held about 112 MiB/s from start to finish: the gigabit link full, for the first time. The slowest link is now the network, and that is exactly where we want it, because it is the one we already improved.

On the cable, 23.5 GiB in 55 seconds give about 440 MiB/s on average, under the 590 that the 5 Gb/s link could carry. The average include the checks, the volume creation and the last memory pass. I did not dig further: one minute, Ludo find that correct.

There was one detail left, and it was mine: `vm-evacuate` still ranked stella and vega as slow-disk hosts. The host table carried a `fastDisk` field exactly for that, with a comment that said to flip it when the NVMes arrive. They arrived, and I opened the fix the same evening. With the four hosts fast, the cable decide first, then free memory: a VM leaving vega will go to stella first.

## The bench, done again properly, and filmed

The earlier numbers were mixing a bit of everything: worker4 before the NVMe, worker4 after, other VMs for the switch, and vega working for others during some runs. Ludo, he asked to do the bench again with the four cases side by side: SSD to SSD, NVMe to NVMe, through the cable and through the switch.

So, a test VM, built for the occasion. No system inside: only a 60 GiB qcow2 disk with 18.8 GiB of random data, and 4 GiB of memory never touched. A VM without a system still run, it wait for a boot disk that will never come, and for the migration it is exactly the same. Two copies: `banc-ssd`, whose disk is on the A400, and `banc-nvme`, on the SN730. Each run do the trip stella → vega then the return, with `virsh migrate --live --copy-storage-all`, the same call as `vm-move`.

The `banc` script that launch them also watch the receiving host: every minute, what its disk wrote and what its link received. It is the assembly line from the previous chapter, measured while she is running.

Three things you can read in this table, and the third one, she caught me by surprise.

**On NVMe, the network decide, and the cable win by four.** The two directions are identical to the second. Through the switch, the receiving host get 1.0 Gb/s and write 107 MiB/s: the gigabit link is full, the disk is waiting. Through the cable, 42 seconds.

**Toward stella, on the A400, the cable is useless.** 5 min 43 through the cable, 5 min 23 through the switch: the switch was even a bit faster, which is noise. The measurement lines tell why. During the first 16 seconds, stella write at 115 MiB/s; the next minute, 55, and it does not move from there. That is the SLC cache filling up, live, exactly like Advantech describe. Once full, the disk write at 55 MiB/s, about 0.45 Gb/s: the 1 Gb/s switch is more than enough to feed it, and the 5 Gb/s cable wait with her.

**Toward vega, the same A400 wrote at 228 MiB/s.** This one, I did not expect. Same disk model, same VM, and four times faster than toward stella. The difference is the state of the two disks. vega's A400 was resting for hours, with no VM on vega, and its SLC cache was empty: 18.8 GiB went in almost in one go. stella's one had just received the 18.8 GiB of the test disk, that I created a little before on that same SSD. Its cache was probably already full before the start. On the second trip toward vega, through the switch, its A400 was writing only 92 MiB/s, under the 107 the NVMe pulled from the same link: its cache too had probably started to fill.

I accused my bench first. My bench had done nothing: it is the disk that does not have the same speed depending on what you asked it before. For an entry-level SSD, "how long to move a VM" does not have one answer. It depend on what it wrote in the last hour.

One last observation, to be complete. Even on NVMe, the cable does not climb to 4.95 Gb/s: the receiving host get 3.7 Gb/s and write about 450 MiB/s, while the SN730 write 1289 under fio. So it is not the disk anymore, and it is not the cable. My hypothesis: the disk copy go through one single NBD connection, and one single TCP stream top out before the link. I present it as a hypothesis, I did not prove it.

## And PCIe 1.0, which changed nothing

Just before this bench, another session found a funny thing in `lspci`. The Kingston NVMes of bob and luna were running in PCIe 1.0: 2.5 GT/s, with `LnkSta` showing `(downgraded)`. On the same motherboards, the SN730s of stella and vega held PCIe 3.0, at 8 GT/s.

At luna, the disk was in the bottom M.2 connector. Ludo moved it to the top one, like on the three other servers, and the link came back up in PCIe 3.0. At bob, the disk was already in the right connector: it came back to PCIe 3.0 after a real 12-minute shutdown, without anybody touching anything. Its previous boot came after a two-minute restart. The hypothesis, not proven, is that a warm restart leave that disk on a failed negotiation.

Before putting everything back in order, Claude Code did the reverse test: force the NVMes to PCIe 1.0 at both ends, and migrate real VMs.

| VM | Trip | Cable | Switch |
|---|---|---|---|
| coquille (35 GiB) | luna → bob | 2 min 29 | 9 min 21 |
| win11 (37 GiB) | vega → stella | 1 min 43 | 7 min 30 |

Nothing visible: through the cable, these durations are in the same range as in PCIe 3.0, and through the switch, the gigabit is what limit, like always. The math explain why. [Kingston's infographic](https://media.kingston.com/pdfs/NVME%20Infographic_MK1701062_EN_0217.pdf) give 500 MB/s per lane in PCIe 2.0 and 1000 MB/s in PCIe 3.0. Gen 1 run at half the rate of Gen 2, 2.5 GT/s against 5 (that is what `lspci` show), so roughly 250 MB/s per lane. An NVMe use four lanes: even in PCIe 1.0, it keep about 1 GB/s.

And the fastest link we saw in this whole story is the cable, which deliver at best 3.7 Gb/s in a migration, about 450 MiB/s. PCIe 1.0 let through more than double. For the Kingstons of bob and luna it is even clearer: their flash write about 280 MB/s sustained. That number, by the way, was probably measured while they were running in PCIe 1.0, and it change nothing to what it say. Even at a quarter of its speed, the connector stayed faster than the flash behind it.

It is the same lesson as everything else, taken from the other end: a link slowed down four times cost nothing if it was not the slowest one.

## What I keep

-   **Measure each link before buying the next one.** `iperf3` told the truth: the cable did 4.95 Gb/s. It said nothing about the migration, which have three links. A measurement of the disk, before the cable, would have saved the surprise.
-   **A migration without shared storage go at the speed of the receiving disk, then of the network.** In the corollary I wrote that the duration is (written disk + memory) divided by the link's throughput. It was true at 1 Gb/s toward an NVMe. It was no longer true at 5 Gb/s toward an entry-level SATA SSD.
-   **"NVMe" name a connector, not a speed.** bob's entry-level Kingston write at about 280 MB/s, the SN730 at about 1300. The flash and its DRAM make the difference, not the four letters; even brought down to PCIe 1.0, the connector was never the slow link.
-   **An entry-level SSD does not have one speed, it have two.** The one of its cache, then the one of its flash. The same A400 wrote at 228 MiB/s rested and at 55 MiB/s after 19 GiB: a bench that does not say what the disk was just doing says nothing.
-   **When two things change at the same time, I do not know which one counted.** I blamed the parallel transfer because it was there. Ludo asked for a bench with one variable at a time, and the bench showed the disk.

The 5 G cable stayed plugged in the whole time, waiting politely for its turn. It was doing exactly what we asked. It is just that nobody, before tonight, was giving it enough work.
