I spent the last few weeks trying different brains for Bob, this blog's chatbot and my home's voice assistant. Most of the blog's articles are written with Anthropic's models, through Claude Code, but for Bob, I wanted a local model: what people say to him does not leave my house, and once the cards are paid for, every question is free. Enter Qwen. American players still dominate the market for proprietary models, but several Chinese players now publish quality models, freely, on Hugging Face, and Qwen, from Alibaba, is one of them.

Along the way, I learned a lesson that goes beyond Bob, and that is what I want to share here: a local model has two speeds, and for a voice assistant, the more expensive of the two depends almost entirely on how you manage its cache. I will show you those two speeds, the benchmark that measures them, what the cache keeps from one question to the next, the two traps I fell into, and the rules I take from it. The work was done in sessions with Claude Code, Opus 5 for the first benchmarks, Opus 5.5 for the rest. As usual, Bob wrote [his corollary](/en/blog/trois-cartes-deux-cerveaux-une-horloge/), which serves as the theoretical reference, with his point of view as the assistant.

## A bit of context: one model, two jobs

Bob has two jobs, and both go through the same model. He answers the blog's visitors, in the chatbot, from my articles. And he answers at home: "Ok Bob, what's the weather?" to the dining room speaker, or through the Home Assistant app on my phone. Both go through `gpu-01`, a machine in my rack with three 12 GB RTX 3060 cards, where the model is served by Ollama. With 36 GB of cards, I don't have room to keep two large models in memory: the chatbot and the voice share the same one, and each has its own requirements. The chatbot has to be accurate, the voice has to be fast.

I had a choice between two families of models. A mixture-of-experts model, or MoE, splits part of its network into experts, and a router picks, for each token, the few experts that will do the work: my Qwen3.6-35B-A3B has 35 billion parameters, but activates only about 3 billion per token, that is the "A3B" in its name. A dense model, like Qwen3.8-27B, puts all of its parameters to work for every token.

In my benchmarks, the dense model made things up less. The best example is a trick question: "Why did you drop VMware?" Nothing in my articles mentions VMware in my lab, so the right answer is to say it doesn't know. The dense model answered "That's not documented — ask Ludo." two times out of two, and nothing more. The MoE of the time invented a story three times out of four. On the other hand, the dense model writes four times slower. For a chatbot, that is acceptable. For a voice, I had to understand where the time really went.

## Two speeds: reading and writing

Before writing anything, a model reads its whole prompt: the instructions, the description of the house's devices, the history and the question. Only then does it write its answer, one token at a time. These two steps do not have the same limits.

To write, the card has to read again, for every token, all the weights that do the work. Memory is the limit: an RTX 3060 reads about 360 GB per second. For the dense model's 16.5 GB, that gives a ceiling of about 22 tokens per second; we measure 18. The MoE, which only reads its active experts, writes 77. And adding a card changes nothing: Ollama splits the model by layers, and the cards work one after the other. On three cards, the MoE wrote between 70 and 72 tokens per second, just like on two.

A question I asked myself while writing this article: if the weights never change, why does the card have to read them again for every token? Because they do not fit where the computing happens. The card's compute units have, in total, only about ten megabytes of fast memory, while the weights take 16,500 and stay in video memory. For every token, the card loads one layer's weights, does its computation, throws them out to make room for the next layer, and starts over at the next token. The weights are the function; the input is what you give it: the text becomes vectors that go through the same layers, and it is because those vectors differ that two questions get two different answers. The cache, for its part, holds no weights: it keeps what was computed from the input. Bob [walks through the whole trip](/en/blog/trois-cartes-deux-cerveaux-une-horloge/), from the word to the vector and from the vector to the next word.

To read, it is the opposite: all the tokens of the prompt are known in advance, the card processes them in batches, and computing power is the limit. The dense model reads 834 tokens per second, the MoE 2,778.

Here is the benchmark in one table, measured on `gpu-01`:

| | MoE 35B-A3B | Dense 27B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parameters at work for each token | about 3 billion of 35 | 27 billion |
| Writing | 77 tokens/s | 18 tokens/s |
| Reading the prompt | 2,778 tokens/s | 834 tokens/s |
| Voice prompt re-read in full (about 5,200 tokens) | 2.1 s | 6.4 s |
| Same prompt, cache intact | 0.15 to 0.25 s | 0.2 to 0.5 s |
| 300-token answer in the chatbot | about 4 s | about 17 s |
| Video memory used | about 23 GB | about 19 GB |
| Trick question (VMware) | invents 3 times out of 4 | "not documented", 2 times out of 2 |

_Speeds measured on September 25, 2026 (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and Qwen3.8-27B, quantized to 4 bits), voice prompt times taken from Ollama's logs on real questions from the house; trick question from the September 13 bench, against Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. The 300-token answer is computed from the writing speed._

The table says it all. For a voice, writing speed matters little: a spoken answer is one or two sentences. What matters is the time before the first word, and that time is reading the prompt. On the dense model, re-reading the whole voice prompt costs 6.4 seconds; with the cache intact, less than half a second. So the whole question becomes: what keeps the cache intact?

## The cache: what a model keeps from one question to the next

When Ollama reads a prompt, it keeps the result of that computation in memory. On the next question, it compares the new prompt with the old one, from the beginning, and reuses everything that is identical. At the first difference, it computes everything after it again, even if the rest is the same. That is the rule to remember: the cache only keeps the common beginning.

If you have ever written a Dockerfile, you already know this principle. Docker builds an image in layers, one per instruction, and keeps each one in its cache. Its [documentation](https://docs.docker.com/build/cache/) says it in one sentence: if a layer changes, all the layers that come after it are affected too. That is why you copy the dependency list and install the dependencies before copying the code, as in [Docker's own example](https://docs.docker.com/build/cache/optimize/):

```dockerfile
FROM node
WORKDIR /app
COPY package.json yarn.lock .    # rarely changes
RUN npm install                  # stays cached
COPY . .                         # changes with every commit
RUN npm build
```

A prompt works the same way. The instructions and the description of the devices are the dependencies: they rarely change, they go at the top. The time, the weather and the question are the code: they change every time, they go at the bottom. Putting the time at the top of the prompt is like doing `COPY . .` on the first line: every build reinstalls everything.

My models add a subtlety, and the comparison still holds. With a classic model, every token is a layer: the server resumes exactly where the difference starts. Recent Qwen models are hybrid: in three of the network's layers out of four, the model keeps a kind of summary of what it has read, and a summary cannot be rewound. It is like a Dockerfile with only a few big layers. The server takes checkpoints in a few places only, at the start of the question and a little more than 1,000 tokens before the end of the prompt, and can only resume from one of them. If something changes in the last big layer, it redoes it whole; if it changes higher up, it redoes everything. The why, with the code to back it, is in [Bob's article](/en/blog/trois-cartes-deux-cerveaux-une-horloge/).

## First trap: what changes at the top of the prompt

In my lab, the first pass through the model took 2.4 to 3.3 seconds on every voice question, on the MoE. I asked Claude Code to read the Home Assistant and Ollama logs to understand why, and to see whether the dense model could become viable with some adjustments.

Those lines, I was the one who had added them to my prompt, so that Bob would know the weather without having to look it up: the current temperature, the date with the time to the minute, and the forecast for the next few days. They changed with every question, and they sat before the description of the house's devices and tools, which makes up most of the prompt. All of it was re-read every time.

The fix comes down to three moves:

-   the three lines are removed from the prompt;
-   the weather becomes a tool, a small script Bob calls when he is asked about the temperature or rain, like he already does for movie showtimes;
-   only the date is left near the beginning, and it changes once a day.

On the MoE, re-reading the prompt went from 2.1 s to about 0.15 s per question. On the dense model, it takes 0.2 to 0.5 s, and that is what made it possible for the voice. The blog's chatbot already had its article excerpts at the end of its prompt, after the part that does not change: it did not have this problem.

To show you the mechanism for real, here is the same experiment replayed on the dense model. A small script sends the same prompt three times in a row, about 3,500 tokens, with the time to the second placed in three spots: at the top of the system prompt, at the end of the system prompt, then inside the question itself. In the middle, the server's log says how many tokens it had to read again.

The three cases, plainly:

-   the time at the top of the system prompt: 3,483 tokens re-read on every question, 4.9 s;
-   the time at the end of the system prompt: 1,024 tokens re-read, 2.3 s;
-   the time inside the question: 29 tokens re-read, 0.37 s.

The middle case is the most instructive: the time at the end of the system prompt is not enough. The model can only resume from a checkpoint, and the closest one was a little more than 1,000 tokens before the end: that is the last big layer, redone whole on every question. What changes has to go in the question itself, or in a tool's answer.

## Second trap: one prompt per device

Even with a stable prompt, the first question at the dining room speaker was sometimes still slow: up to 8.3 seconds for the first pass through the model, which recomputed its 5,189 tokens from zero.

Claude Code first blamed the blog's chatbot, a lead we had already ruled out. It was by measuring again that it found the real cause: the same question, asked with and without the speaker's identity. Without it, 0.8 s. With it, 7.4 s, then 0.8 s on the next question. A question coming from the speaker does not produce the same prompt as a question from the app: Home Assistant adds the device's room and its timers. And the warm-up I ran every morning had no way to say which device it came from. It kept the app's prompt warm, not the dining room's.

The fix is a small service on another machine in the lab, which asks Bob a harmless question every hour, once on behalf of the speaker, once with no device. Both prompts stay ready.

## The rules I take from it

1.  **Measure the two speeds separately.** The time before the first word and the writing speed have neither the same causes nor the same remedies. A faster card helps writing; the cache helps reading.
2.  **What changes goes after what does not.** The instructions and the description of the devices first; the time, the weather and the question at the end. Just like a Dockerfile: dependencies before code.
3.  **On a hybrid model, "at the end" means in the question or in a tool's answer.** The end of the system prompt is not enough.
4.  **What rarely changes can stay at the top.** The date changes once a day: one full re-read per day is an acceptable price.
5.  **Warm up the exact prompt.** A warm-up that does not build the same prompt, device included, warms up nothing.
6.  **Read the server's log.** Ollama's API always reports the full size of the prompt, even when almost everything comes from the cache. The number of tokens really re-read is in llama-server's log.

## Day to day

With the cache managed well, here is what my usual questions give on the dense model, measured from the text sent to the written answer, without speech recognition or synthesis:

-   "what day is it", "who are you", "what time is it": 1.2 to 2.5 s;
-   the temperature, the weather, the rain, the status of a light: 3.3 to 4.9 s, because Bob calls a tool, then writes his answer;
-   a web search or the list of movies playing: 7.5 to 11 s, mostly because of the length of the answer.

We tried letting Home Assistant answer simple commands itself, without going through the model: the weather answered in 0.1 s, but with fixed wording, without Bob's personality. I decided: "No, actually I'd rather have everything go through Bob, it was fast enough." For the voice itself, I suggested Piper, a local voice: "We could maybe use Piper for Bob, since he's a bot, no need to be too human?" Unlike the Google voice I was using, Piper speaks as a stream: it starts reading the first sentence while Bob writes the rest.

The blog's chatbot, for its part, pays the price of the dense model. At 18 tokens per second, a long 300-token answer takes about 17 seconds to write, against 4 with the old model. Most of Bob's answers are shorter, but that is the trade-off: a slower chatbot, which makes things up less.

## Final word

In the end, for short questions, the slowest model answers the voice as fast as the old one did before the fix, a bit slower when it has to call a tool, and it makes things up less. It was not an extra card that made the difference, it was the order of a few lines in a prompt I had written myself.

Eventually, I would like to replace the 3060s with 24 GB RTX 3090 cards, which read their memory about 2.6 times faster, and the machine already has a 1,500 W power supply. That would help writing. Reading was already solved by the cache. Maybe a topic for another time.

So if your local model feels slow, I would tell you to look at what changes at the top of your prompt before looking at the price of cards.
